# Industry-Grade Simulation: Closing the Gap Between GridPilot and Real Fleet-Charging Systems

**Date:** 2026-07-18
**Scope:** Cited research on (1) commercial fleet-charging optimization architecture, (2) tractable battery-physics models for simulation, (3) grid/DISCOM validation practice, and (4) India-specific data-fidelity expectations — benchmarked against GridPilot's current single-shot QP architecture.

---

## Executive Summary

GridPilot currently runs a **single-shot, day-ahead CVXPY QP** over a fixed 96-slot horizon with no re-solving, no battery physics beyond a linear power bound, real-power-only grid constraints, and synthetic session/grid/carbon data. Every one of these choices diverges from what the commercial and academic state of the art treats as baseline practice:

1. **Architecture.** Real managed-charging platforms (WeaveGrid's DISCO, PowerFlex's Adaptive Load Management, Kaluza Flex) run as continuously-updating dispatch loops, not single overnight solves — WeaveGrid explicitly distinguishes "static" day-ahead programs from "dynamic" real-time optimization programs that re-derive schedules as new telemetry and grid-constraint data arrive ([WeaveGrid/Xcel case, energychangemakers.com](https://energychangemakers.com/weavegrid-ev-charging/)). Academic literature converges on **receding-horizon / MPC control** re-solved every 5 minutes to a few hours, frequently paired with a two-stage day-ahead + lightweight intraday re-optimization pattern ([DTU ISGT 2024](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/378883659/Receding_horizon_paper_ISGT_2024_conference_reviewed_no_IEEE_number.pdf); [Shen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/automation6040064)). Forecast uncertainty is handled via scenario-based stochastic optimization, robust optimization with an uncertainty budget, or conformal-prediction-driven stochastic MPC — treating forecasts as ground truth (GridPilot's current approach) is explicitly what these papers are correcting for.

2. **Battery physics.** A free power variable bounded only by charger rating (GridPilot's model) is a known oversimplification but not an unreasonable *first* simplification — the literature's actual fix is cheap: **SOC-dependent (piecewise-linear or concave) maximum-charging-power constraints** that approximate CC→CV tapering, addable to a QP/LP with 3-10 breakpoints at negligible solve-time cost and demonstrated <0.35% cost/schedule error versus exact nonlinear models ([Schaden et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/en14227755); [Butt & Li 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18853)). Degradation cost terms are commonly linearized (piecewise-linear cost-per-kWh-throughput, sometimes SOC/C-rate/temperature-weighted) and added directly to the QP objective ([Hemmati et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.est.2026.121551)).

3. **Grid validation.** Real depot/DISCOM interactions are dominated by **demand-charge structures and simultaneous-plug-in spikes**, not by reactive-power physics — real-power-only modeling is treated as an accepted simplification in the fleet-ops literature (concurrency-factor / demand-charge framing dominates: [Fleetvolts 2025](https://fleetvolts.com/blog/avoiding-demand-charge-spikes-depot)), while reactive power/power-factor omission is flagged as a gap mainly in *distribution-network voltage/loss* studies, not depot-level fleet dispatch ones — a genuine but second-order gap for GridPilot's use case.

4. **India-specific credibility.** The single most consequential fidelity gap for DISCOM stakeholder credibility is **synthetic EV session data** and **synthetic grid/IEX demand data** — India's actual DISCOM pilots (BYPL/eDRV three-wheeler pilot, BRPL V2G pilot) and the Fraunhofer/IITB/CEA-adjacent smart-charging study explicitly built on real feeder and session data because DISCOMs evaluate strategies on "practicability" and existing measurement infrastructure ([Changing Transport / Fraunhofer-IITB-DTU report, 2024](https://changing-transport.org/wp-content/uploads/2024_Recommendation-for-Deployment-of-Smart-Charging-for-Electric-Vehicles-in-India.pdf)). Hardcoded carbon intensity is a comparatively minor gap — CEA emission factors are official and widely used as-is in Indian analyses.

The report below details each finding with sources, flags single-source/unverified claims, and closes with five ranked, effort/payoff-scored recommendations.

## 1. How real commercial fleet/depot smart-charging systems architect optimization + simulation

### 1.1 MPC / receding-horizon vs. single-shot day-ahead

**WeaveGrid (DISCO / EVMS)** — the most-documented commercial platform found — explicitly separates two operating modes for utility programs: **static programs** built around fixed time-of-use rates that barely change once set, and **dynamic, real-time distribution-optimization programs** that produce materially different, continuously-updated charge schedules as local transformer/feeder conditions change ([energychangemakers.com interview with WeaveGrid](https://energychangemakers.com/weavegrid-ev-charging/)). In the Xcel Energy Colorado program, WeaveGrid ingests day-ahead price/wind signals and issues schedules "the next evening" — i.e., a rolling day-ahead cadence, not a single seasonal or one-time solve. DISCO's core function is described as translating live grid constraints (transformer/feeder/bulk-system limits) into per-device dispatch instructions continuously ([WeaveGrid–PowerFlex partnership announcement](https://www.weavegrid.com/news/weavegrid-and-powerflex-announce-partnership)) — this is a receding-horizon dispatch pattern even though WeaveGrid doesn't use the term "MPC" publicly. This is **marketing/product material, not a peer-reviewed architecture disclosure** — treat the exact re-solve cadence as approximate/unverified beyond "day-ahead or faster."

**PowerFlex's Adaptive Load Management (ALM)**, the charger-side complement in that partnership, modulates power **in real time** across ports based on fleet schedules, site constraints, and driver requirements ([WeaveGrid/PowerFlex](https://www.weavegrid.com/news/weavegrid-and-powerflex-announce-partnership)) — confirming a two-layer architecture (utility/grid-facing dispatch layer + site-level real-time control layer), a pattern GridPilot's single QP does not have at all.

**Kaluza Flex** (OVO Energy spinout) runs a continuously-updating optimization engine on Google Cloud: real-time vehicle/charger telemetry streams through a data backbone into an "optimization engine" that computes charge schedules per plug-in event, with BigQuery/Looker dashboards for grid operators ([Kaluza/Google Cloud case study](https://sustainablefuture.cio.com/building-on-intelligent-infrastructure/kaluza-driving-the-energy-transition-with-electric-vehicle-charging/)). Again, this is vendor material — no published re-solve interval — but it corroborates a "recompute per event / continuously" pattern rather than one nightly batch solve.

**Fermata Energy's V2X software** similarly frames itself as continuously analyzing rate/demand/weather/driver-pattern data streams to identify revenue-optimal charge/discharge windows ([Fermata Energy FAQ](https://fermataenergy.com/faq/how-does-fermata-energys-v2x-software-work/)) — consistent with the same pattern but again without disclosed technical cadence (**single-source, marketing-level claim**).

**Academic corroboration for receding-horizon control specifically at charging-station scale:**
- A DTU (Technical University of Denmark) 2024 ISGT paper implements a genuine receding-horizon MILP for EV charging-station control, re-solving **every 5 minutes** over a 6-hour look-ahead, rolling forward continuously, with only the current-step decision applied each cycle (classic MPC pattern) — delivering >90% energy-request fulfillment while lowering cost ([DTU 2024](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/378883659/Receding_horizon_paper_ISGT_2024_conference_reviewed_no_IEEE_number.pdf)).
- A 2025 paper from TU Dresden/Deutsche Telekom researchers on **fleet** charging (closest single match to GridPilot's use case: workplace/depot fleet + PV) explicitly proposes a **two-stage** pattern: a day-ahead MILP from forecasts, followed by an **event-driven, lightweight intraday MILP re-optimization** triggered by unplanned arrivals or forecast error, validated in a Python simulator under stochastic PV/load/price/EV conditions ([Shen et al. 2025, Automation journal](https://doi.org/10.3390/automation6040064)). This is the most directly transferable architecture pattern for GridPilot: keep the nightly day-ahead QP, but add a cheap intraday re-optimization trigger.
- Morstyn et al.'s SOCP-based EV charging formulation explicitly implements it as a "receding horizon implementation [that] allows the charging schedule to be updated online, without requiring prior information about when vehicles will arrive" ([Morstyn et al. 2020, IEEE TTE](https://doi.org/10.1109/tte.2020.2986675)).
- A 2026 arXiv paper comparing a heuristic sequential-DP fleet solver against a full-fleet Gurobi MILP solver for monthly-horizon smart charging of large fleets shows re-solve/tractability is a **real, current** research concern for fleet-scale (not just single-EV) scheduling — sequential DP trades ~9% cost sub-optimality for large tractability gains at fleet scale ([Kuvvetli et al. 2026, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.20067)).

**Re-solve cadence, synthesized across sources:** commercial platforms appear to operate on a spectrum from **event-triggered (per plug-in) to daily**, while academic receding-horizon EV-charging-station papers most commonly use **5-minute to hourly** re-solve intervals with 6-24 hour look-ahead windows. No single source gives one canonical "industry standard" cadence — this is a **range**, not a fixed number, and depot/fleet-specific papers (as opposed to single-station papers) lean toward the day-ahead + event-driven-intraday pattern rather than 5-minute MPC, likely because fleet SOC/arrival dynamics change more slowly than single-charger dispatch.

### 1.2 Forecast uncertainty handling

Four distinct approaches recur in recent literature, and none of them is "treat the forecast as ground truth":

- **Scenario-based stochastic optimization with quantile forecasts**, applied specifically to fleet-level day-ahead market bidding, models the fleet as a "virtual battery" and generates scenarios from quantile forecasts of arrival/departure/SOC and price, then optimizes with **Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)** to control downside tail risk from forecast error ([Utrecht Univ. research portal paper](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/231890309/Scheduling_Electric_Vehicle_Fleets_as_a_Virtual_Battery_under_Uncertainty_using_Quantile_Forecasts.pdf)).
- **Robust optimization with an uncertainty budget** in a receding-horizon EV smart-charging setting quantifies a "price of robustness" against PV, load, arrival-SOC, energy-need, and arrival/departure-time uncertainty — finding EV driving-pattern uncertainty (arrival/departure/SOC) is far more damaging to delivered-energy performance than PV/load uncertainty, while the cost of hedging against it ("price of robustness") is low ([Damianakis et al. 2024, PEMC](https://doi.org/10.1109/pemc61721.2024.10726368)). This is directly relevant to GridPilot: its arrival-time/energy-need forecasts (currently ground truth) are exactly the uncertainty category shown to matter most.
- **Scenario-based stochastic MPC with conformal-prediction-calibrated probabilistic forecasts** — a 2025 TU Eindhoven paper builds an EMS that generates ML-based point forecasts, wraps them in distribution-free conformal-prediction confidence intervals, derives scenario trees, and re-solves a stochastic second-order-cone MPC every re-solve step; in a 10-day closed-loop evaluation, the stochastic MPC beat a deterministic point-forecast MPC by only ~0.1% cost — i.e., **the marginal value of stochastic vs. deterministic re-solving was small once re-solving itself was in place**, but both meaningfully outperform doing nothing ([Fernández-Zapico et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.23919/ecc65951.2025.11187078)). This is a useful nuance: the big win is *re-solving with updated point forecasts*, not necessarily full stochastic optimization.
- **Large-scale scenario-based multi-objective MILP** for 500-EV V2G fleets uses Monte Carlo scenario generation for PV forecast error (±25%) and EV availability error (±40%) directly in the optimization, alongside degradation and lifecycle-emission terms ([Gül & Kökçam 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19102398)).

**Synthesis:** the dominant practical pattern combines (a) periodic re-solving with updated point forecasts (captures most of the value cheaply) with (b) scenario/robust methods layered on top mainly to bound downside risk on the *most volatile* inputs (EV availability/SOC, not weather/price). GridPilot currently does neither.

## 2. Tractable battery-physics models for fleet-level charging simulation

### 2.1 CC-CV tapering approximation

The core finding across multiple 2024-2025 papers: full nonlinear CC-CV / electrochemical modeling is unnecessary for scheduling-grade fidelity — **SOC-dependent maximum-charging-power constraints**, represented as a piecewise-linear or concave function of SOC, capture the practically relevant effect (charging power tapers as the battery approaches full) at negligible computational cost:

- A 2025 paper proposes exactly this for BESS-style planning models: **SOC-dependent Tapered Charging Power (TCP) constraints** approximating CCCV behavior, showing that ignoring tapering leads to *undersized* battery/charger provisioning and reliability issues in planning studies ([Butt & Li 2025, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18853)).
- Schaden et al. formalize this as reformulating the scheduling problem in terms of *maximum chargeable energy per timestep* (rather than power), derived from a SOC-power curve, and show two solvable forms: an LP-compatible **concave piecewise-linear** approximation, and a more general (possibly non-concave) piecewise-linear form requiring a MILP with binary segment-selection variables. Critically, they benchmark accuracy: **5-10 linear segments produce negligible charging-cost differences (<0.35% mean error) versus the true nonlinear curve**, and recommend this approach specifically for use inside a model-predictive-control (i.e., receding-horizon) re-optimization loop with small time steps ([Schaden, Jatschka, Limmer & Raidl 2021, Energies](https://doi.org/10.3390/en14227755)).
- A companion IEEE paper on the same question found the **SoC-dependent max-power constraint had negligible impact** relative to grid/charger power caps for many scenarios (final mean SoC deviation <0.2%) because the fleet-level grid cap binds before the per-vehicle taper does — suggesting for GridPilot's 40-vehicle/transformer-limited setup, the taper matters more at low fleet utilization (few vehicles near 80%+ SOC simultaneously) than at high utilization ([IEEE paper on SoC-dependent max charging power](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/ielx7/6687316/7098407/10044725.pdf)).
- For DC fast-charging-specific fidelity, Morstyn et al. model the CC-CV effect via a **reduced-order battery circuit model** (open-circuit voltage + internal resistance) formulated as **second-order cone constraints**, integrated into an SOCP that remains polynomial-time solvable — a heavier but still convex/tractable option if GridPilot ever needs per-cell voltage fidelity ([Morstyn et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/tte.2020.2986675)).
- A bilevel-programming approach for fitting optimal piecewise-linear breakpoints to real charge-curve data, then embedding the fit into a MILP, solves for 200 simultaneous EVs in under 2.5 seconds — evidence this pattern scales well beyond GridPilot's 40-vehicle fleet ([Saner, Saha & Srinivasan 2024, ISGT Europe](https://doi.org/10.1109/isgteurope62998.2024.10863354)).

**Practical takeaway for GridPilot:** replacing the current flat charger-power bound with a 3-5-segment piecewise-linear SOC-dependent power cap (steep taper above ~80% SOC per manufacturer-typical CC-CV curves) is a **low-effort QP-compatible addition** (piecewise-linear constraints remain convex/QP-solvable with auxiliary variables) with literature-verified <1% cost/error impact — a genuine, cheap credibility improvement.

### 2.2 Thermal derating

This is the weakest-evidenced area in the research: search results were dominated by nonlinear MPC / dynamic-programming approaches for **single-cell fast-charging thermal management** (relevant to onboard BMS design, not fleet-dispatch scheduling), e.g. integrated NMPC+DP thermal-management strategies achieving near-optimal fast-charge profiles while respecting temperature limits ([arXiv 2404.04358](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.04358); [Springer 2025 predictive battery thermal management](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41104-025-00157-7)). None of the fleet-scheduling papers reviewed here incorporate an explicit ambient-temperature derating curve into a *fleet-level dispatch* objective/constraint — thermal derating appears to be handled at the charger/BMS firmware level (invisible to the scheduler) in essentially all fleet-scale literature found. **This should be flagged as an open gap rather than asserted as standard practice** — I could not find a published fleet-scheduling QP/MILP that includes an explicit ambient-temperature-dependent power cap term. Given Gurugram's summer ambient temperatures materially exceed the 15-35°C range most CC-CV/thermal papers assume as "nominal," this is a legitimate open question rather than a solved one GridPilot is simply skipping.

### 2.3 Degradation cost terms

Two clear, tractable patterns:

- **Linearized semi-empirical aging cost added directly to the MILP/QP objective.** Hemmati et al. (2026) build an "Aging Price" metric from a semi-empirical nonlinear cycle-aging model (function of temperature, C-rate, and energy throughput), linearize it via piecewise-linear approximation, and solve iteratively (since aging price depends on temperature, which depends on the charge/discharge pattern the optimizer is choosing) ([Hemmati, Farzin & Monadi 2026, J. Energy Storage](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.est.2026.121551)). This is heavier than GridPilot needs today but shows the standard pattern: linearize degradation as $/kWh-throughput (or $/cycle), add as a linear objective term, iterate if temperature-coupling is included.
- **Large-scale scenario MILP** applies a flat degradation penalty alongside SOH (state-of-health) fade projection and lifecycle-emission (LCA) penalties in a 500-EV V2G fleet objective, treating degradation cost as one of several weighted objective terms in a Pareto-frontier multi-objective formulation ([Gül & Kökçam 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19102398)).

**Practical takeaway for GridPilot:** a simple linear $/kWh-throughput degradation penalty term (even a flat coefficient, not temperature/C-rate-dependent) added to the QP objective would be consistent with the *simplest* end of published practice, and is a low-effort, high-signal addition — it's the difference between "the optimizer treats charging as free" and "the optimizer has *any* concept that fast/full charging has a cost," which is the property DISCOM/industry reviewers are most likely to probe.

## 3. Grid/DISCOM validation in practice vs. simulation

### 3.1 What real depot operators actually manage against

The dominant real-world constraint for fleet depots is **demand charges from simultaneous plug-in**, not steady-state thermal or reactive-power limits. A fleet-electrification-focused source lays this out concretely: a depot's metered draw can jump 60-150 kW within 30 minutes when vehicles return and plug in together, and utilities set the *entire month's* demand charge off that one 15-minute interval — one bad interval can cost $1,000-3,000; staggered/managed charging (reducing the "concurrency factor" from ~0.95-1.0 unmanaged to 0.20-0.40 managed) is presented as the primary lever ([Fleetvolts 2025](https://fleetvolts.com/blog/avoiding-demand-charge-spikes-depot); note this is a single vendor-content source, **US-context, unverified against a second source** — treat concurrency-factor numbers as illustrative, not universal).

For India specifically, the **DISCOM Dilemma** analysis (Observer Research Foundation, May 2026) makes several load-bearing, India-specific claims:
- A 100-bus depot generates 4-5 MW of charging demand; larger depots see 60-70 MW — i.e., fleet depots are treated by DISCOMs as large, lumpy commercial/industrial loads, not diffuse residential load ([ORF 2026](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/the-discom-dilemma-in-india-s-e-bus-transition)).
- India introduced mandatory **Time-of-Day (ToD) tariffs** for all consumers above 10kW from April 2024, extended to all non-agricultural consumers by April 2025 — meaning GridPilot's target depot (a 40-vehicle corporate depot, almost certainly >10kW sanctioned load) is **already subject to a real, dated regulatory tariff structure** that GridPilot's synthetic/simplified cost model does not appear to reference by name.
- **Many DISCOMs still lack smart-metering/digital infrastructure** to actually implement or verify ToD-based demand response — meaning that even a technically perfect optimization schedule may not be *measurable or enforceable* by the DISCOM side today, a real-world friction point a "for DISCOM stakeholders" demo should acknowledge rather than assume away.
- CEA is actively developing V2G/reverse-charging strategies, and a BRPL (Delhi) pilot has already demonstrated AC-based bidirectional V2G export ([ORF 2026](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/the-discom-dilemma-in-india-s-e-bus-transition)) — this is a single-source claim from a policy commentary piece, not a primary CEA/BRPL technical report; flag as **plausible but not independently verified** here.

The NITI Aayog EV Charging Infrastructure Handbook confirms the regulatory chain (CEA sets technical standards; SERCs set tariffs; DISCOMs are the default State Nodal Agency and are responsible for connection, tariff enforcement, and grid upgrades tied to EV load growth) and explicitly calls out **harmonic injection** as a technical grid-quality issue alongside basic capacity, separate from the demand-charge issue ([NITI Aayog EV Handbook](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-02/EV_Handbook_Final_14Oct.pdf)). RMI's 2025 "Plugging In" report corroborates: DISCOMs face **insufficient forecasting/planning tools and limited smart-metering deployment**, and explicitly lists harmonic injection as a reliability risk requiring attention alongside capacity planning ([RMI 2025](https://rmi.org/wp-content/uploads/dlm_uploads/2025/06/rmi-2025-06-plugging-in.pdf)).

### 3.2 Is real-power-only (ignoring reactive power / power factor) an accepted simplification, or a known gap?

The evidence here is **mixed and scope-dependent** — this is the least settled question of the four:

- At the **depot/fleet-dispatch level**, none of the fleet-scheduling papers reviewed (WeaveGrid, PowerFlex, Kaluza, DTU receding-horizon paper, Shen et al. two-stage MILP, Kuvvetli et al. sequential-DP paper) model reactive power explicitly — all formulate constraints in real power (kW) terms. This suggests real-power-only dispatch is indeed the *accepted norm* for fleet/depot-level scheduling optimization specifically.
- At the **distribution-network level** (voltage regulation, line losses, hosting capacity), reactive power is treated as a real and non-trivial gap: a poor power factor (0.95→0.80) is shown to increase line losses by ~35%, energy bills by ~20%, and line currents by ~25% in one analysis, and multiple recent papers explicitly note that "most studies... neglect reactive power," positioning reactive-power-aware dispatch as a genuine, actively-being-closed research gap rather than settled practice ([WebSearch synthesis, unverified against primary sources — see note below]). One paper explicitly assumes EVs operate at unity power factor (zero reactive injection) as a simplifying baseline before extending to reactive-power-aware coordination ([OSTI EV reactive power coordination paper](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1375229)).
- India's NITI/RMI sources single out **harmonic injection** (a related but distinct power-quality issue from reactive power/power factor per se) as a live technical concern DISCOMs are tracking, without giving a specific power-factor or THD threshold.

**Conclusion for GridPilot specifically:** real-power-only modeling at the transformer/sanctioned-load cap level (what GridPilot does) matches accepted practice for *fleet-level dispatch optimization* — this is not the highest-priority gap. Where it becomes a legitimate weakness is if GridPilot's demo claims to validate against the DISCOM's *actual* interconnection/PCC constraints, since DISCOM sanctioned-load and CEA connectivity regulations are stated in kVA (apparent power), and a poor charger-side power factor (older/cheap AC chargers commonly run PF 0.85-0.95 unless corrected) would eat into the kW headroom GridPilot assumes at a fixed 0.8 PF conversion — worth a footnote/sensitivity check rather than a full reactive-power OPF rebuild.

**Flag:** the reactive-power impact statistics above came from a WebSearch synthesis without a single strongly-attributable primary source; treat the 35%/20%/25% figures as **directionally illustrative, not verified**, pending a primary-source read.

## 4. India-specific data-fidelity gaps that matter for DISCOM/utility credibility

### 4.1 Existing India EV-depot / DISCOM smart-charging pilots found

- **BYPL (BSES Yamuna) × eDRV × CEEW pilot, Delhi** (UK PACT-funded): installed 24 smart plugs across two charging stations, ran demand-side-management experiments on **real** three- and four-wheeler EV rickshaw fleets, and found ~13% cost reduction from peak-to-off-peak load shifting — explicitly framed as testing "real-life scenarios of peak demand and network congestion," i.e., built on measured telemetry rather than synthetic sessions ([UK PACT case study](https://www.ukpact.co.uk/case-studies/piloting-ev-charging-in-india)). This is the closest found analogue to GridPilot's use case and demonstrates DISCOMs (BYPL specifically) already engage with pilot-scale real-data managed-charging demonstrations in India.
- **BRPL (BSES Rajdhani) V2G pilot, Delhi**: demonstrated AC-based bidirectional V2G export to the grid — cited only via the ORF commentary piece, **single-source, unverified against a BRPL/CEA primary report**.
- **Fraunhofer IEE / IIT Bombay / DTU / Comillas "NDC-TIA" study**: a multi-institution, government-facing (CEA/CERC/SERC/DISCOM-facing) simulation study explicitly built on **real Indian distribution-feeder models** using the open-source `pandapower` tool, running full-year time-series simulations of uncontrolled vs. smart-charging scenarios across low/medium/high EV-penetration cases, with an explicit evaluation criterion of "practicability" for real-life DISCOM implementation and a scoring dimension for how much *additional measurement/ICT infrastructure* a strategy requires ([Changing Transport / Fraunhofer-IITB-DTU-Comillas report, 2024](https://changing-transport.org/wp-content/uploads/2024_Recommendation-for-Deployment-of-Smart-Charging-for-Electric-Vehicles-in-India.pdf)). This is the single most load-bearing source for Q4: it is explicitly designed to be used by CEA, CERC, SERCs, and DISCOMs to revise policy, and its own methodology bar is "real distribution-system models, not synthetic ones," plus an explicit scoring penalty for strategies requiring measurement infrastructure that doesn't yet exist in most Indian distribution grids.

### 4.2 What data-fidelity gaps matter most, ranked by what these sources emphasize

1. **Synthetic EV session data (arrival times, energy needs, SOC) is the highest-leverage gap.** The Fraunhofer-IITB study's entire "practicability" scoring axis is about whether a strategy can be evaluated/implemented against real infrastructure and real behavior; the BYPL/eDRV pilot's entire value proposition to the DISCOM was demonstrating results on real fleet telemetry, not a lab simulation. A DISCOM reviewer's first question about a synthetic (Caltech ACN-Data-derived) session dataset for a *Gurugram* depot would likely be "does this match observed Indian fleet behavior at all?" — and the honest current answer is "adapted from US public charging statistics," which is a real credibility risk specifically because directly comparable Indian pilot data (BYPL/eDRV) now exists in some form.
2. **Synthetic grid/IEX demand data is the second gap**, because DISCOM tariff and demand-charge exposure (the actual dollars-and-cents stakeholder concern per the ORF and Fleetvolts material) depends on real load-shape/price data, not synthetic profiles — this connects directly to the recommendation to model actual ToD tariff structures (mandatory in India since April 2025) rather than a generic cost proxy.
3. **Hardcoded CEA carbon-intensity factor is a comparatively minor gap.** Using the official CEA Haryana grid-emission factor (0.710 kg/kWh, 2024-25) as a fixed constant is *standard practice* in Indian energy analyses — CEA factors are published exactly for this purpose and are not normally treated as something that needs to be dynamic/real-time for a planning-grade or demo-grade tool. This is not a priority fix.
4. **Real vs. synthetic weather** is a similarly minor gap given GridPilot already uses real Open-Meteo data where available with a documented synthetic NCR fallback — this is closer to accepted practice (graceful degradation) than a red flag, as long as the fallback is disclosed.
5. **DISCOM tariff/penalty structure accuracy** is a meaningful, addressable gap: none of the reviewed India sources suggest GridPilot needs the DISCOM's confidential penalty formulas, but they do converge on **ToD tariff structure** (peak/off-peak multipliers, mandatory since April 2025 for >10kW consumers) as the single most reviewable, checkable, and currently-missing piece of tariff realism for an Indian audience ([ORF 2026](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/the-discom-dilemma-in-india-s-e-bus-transition)).

**Gap acknowledgment:** I could not find a published, primary-source CEA or DISCOM technical guidance document specifically for *fleet depot* smart-charging pilots (as opposed to general EVCI guidelines or e-bus-specific commentary) — the closest primary regulatory document found is the Ministry of Power's 2024 EVCI Guidelines ([yocharge.com hosted PDF](https://yocharge.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Guidelines_and_Standards_for_EVCI_dated_17_09_2024_compressed.pdf)), which covers safety/connectivity/communication-protocol standards but not depot-specific smart-charging simulation-fidelity expectations. Depot-specific DISCOM validation criteria, where they exist, appear to live in state-level SERC supply codes not indexed by general web search — this should be treated as an open research gap, not filled with a plausible-sounding guess.

## Recommendations (ranked)

Each recommendation states what it is, rough effort against GridPilot's actual architecture (`gridpilot/scheduler.py` single-shot CVXPY QP, 96-slot fixed horizon, `gridpilot/ev_manager.py`/`gridpilot/depot_sim.py` fleet/session generation, `pipeline/dvvnl_loader.py` DISCOM grid limits), credibility payoff, and which research question(s) it answers.

### 1. Add a linear degradation cost term + SOC-dependent tapered charging-power constraint to the QP objective/constraints
- **What:** Replace the flat charger-power upper bound with a 3-5-segment piecewise-linear SOC-dependent max-power cap (steep taper above ~80% SOC), and add a flat $/kWh-throughput degradation penalty to the objective.
- **Effort:** **Low.** Both remain LP/QP-compatible (piecewise-linear constraints via auxiliary variables); no solver change, no architecture change — a few dozen lines in `scheduler.py`.
- **Payoff:** **Medium-high.** This is the single cheapest way to move GridPilot's battery model from "no physics" to "literature-standard simplification," and it's the first thing a technically literate reviewer would probe.
- **Answers:** Q2 (directly); indirectly strengthens Q1 credibility (shows awareness of real charging-curve behavior).
- **Sources:** [Schaden et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/en14227755); [Butt & Li 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18853); [Hemmati et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.est.2026.121551).

### 2. Add a day-ahead + event-driven intraday re-optimization layer (not full 5-minute MPC)
- **What:** Keep the nightly 96-slot day-ahead QP solve, but add a lightweight second solve triggered by material events (vehicle arrives off-schedule, forecast updates materially) that re-optimizes only the remaining horizon with updated inputs — following the Shen et al. two-stage pattern rather than continuous MPC.
- **Effort:** **Medium.** Requires refactoring `scheduler.py` to accept a partial/remaining-horizon call and a trigger mechanism (event or periodic re-check), but does not require rebuilding the optimization core, forecast pipeline, or moving to a different solver class.
- **Payoff:** **High.** This is the single largest architectural gap versus every commercial and academic source reviewed — single-shot day-ahead-only optimization with zero re-solving is the one design choice with no supporting precedent anywhere in the research (even the simplest real systems re-solve at least daily, with intraday adjustment on top).
- **Answers:** Q1 (directly).
- **Sources:** [WeaveGrid](https://energychangemakers.com/weavegrid-ev-charging/); [Shen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/automation6040064); [DTU 2024](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/378883659/Receding_horizon_paper_ISGT_2024_conference_reviewed_no_IEEE_number.pdf).

### 3. Replace/augment synthetic EV session data with real or India-benchmarked telemetry
- **What:** Either obtain/license real Indian fleet-depot telemetry (or a proxy dataset closer to Indian usage patterns than Caltech ACN-Data, e.g. parameters calibrated against the BYPL/eDRV pilot's published statistics), or explicitly recalibrate the synthetic-session generator's arrival-time/energy-need distributions against published Indian pilot statistics rather than US public-charging statistics.
- **Effort:** **Medium-high.** Getting real telemetry requires an external data-sharing relationship (out of GridPilot's control); recalibrating synthetic generation against published Indian pilot summary statistics (BYPL/eDRV 13% peak-shift savings, arrival-time clustering patterns described in the Fraunhofer-IITB report) is feasible with just the `ev_manager.py`/`depot_sim.py` generators and public report data, without needing raw data access.
- **Payoff:** **High for DISCOM audiences specifically.** This is the single gap most likely to be raised first by an Indian utility stakeholder, since directly comparable Indian pilot data now exists publicly and "synthetic, US-derived" is an easy, specific critique to make.
- **Answers:** Q4 (directly).
- **Sources:** [UK PACT/eDRV/BYPL pilot](https://www.ukpact.co.uk/case-studies/piloting-ev-charging-in-india); [Fraunhofer/IITB/DTU/Comillas report](https://changing-transport.org/wp-content/uploads/2024_Recommendation-for-Deployment-of-Smart-Charging-for-Electric-Vehicles-in-India.pdf).

### 4. Model India's mandatory Time-of-Day (ToD) tariff structure explicitly, replace synthetic IEX/grid-demand data with a documented real or realistic tariff/price proxy
- **What:** Encode the actual ToD peak/off-peak tariff multipliers (mandatory for >10kW consumers since April 2025) into the cost objective in place of/alongside synthetic grid-demand-derived pricing, and clearly label any remaining synthetic IEX component as such in outputs.
- **Effort:** **Low-medium.** ToD tariff schedules are publicly published by Haryana's SERC/DVVNL tariff orders — this is a lookup-and-encode task in `pipeline/dvvnl_loader.py` and `gridpilot/scheduler.py`'s cost objective, not a new subsystem.
- **Payoff:** **Medium-high.** Directly addresses the ORF/RMI-identified reality that ToD tariffs are now the primary near-term DISCOM/fleet economic interaction in India, and it's independently checkable by a DISCOM reviewer (unlike synthetic IEX data).
- **Answers:** Q3 and Q4.
- **Sources:** [ORF DISCOM Dilemma 2026](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/the-discom-dilemma-in-india-s-e-bus-transition); [RMI 2025](https://rmi.org/wp-content/uploads/dlm_uploads/2025/06/rmi-2025-06-plugging-in.pdf).

### 5. Add a forecast-uncertainty sensitivity/robustness layer around arrival time, energy need, and SOC (not weather/price)
- **What:** Rather than full stochastic/scenario optimization, add a robust-optimization-style uncertainty budget or simple scenario-perturbation sensitivity analysis specifically on EV arrival-time, energy-need, and SOC forecasts — the inputs literature shows matter most — leaving weather/price forecasts as point estimates (low marginal value per the Eindhoven stochastic-MPC finding).
- **Effort:** **Medium.** Robust-optimization reformulation of existing constraints (uncertainty-budget style) is a bounded, well-documented extension to a QP; a simpler sensitivity-analysis version (re-run the existing solver under perturbed EV-side inputs, report schedule stability) is low effort and could ship first.
- **Payoff:** **Medium.** Less visible to non-technical stakeholders than Recommendations 1-4, but directly closes a specific, named gap ("forecasts treated as ground truth") and is cheap to demonstrate qualitatively even without full implementation.
- **Answers:** Q1 (directly).
- **Sources:** [Damianakis et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/pemc61721.2024.10726368); [Fernández-Zapico et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.23919/ecc65951.2025.11187078); [Utrecht virtual-battery quantile-forecast paper](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/231890309/Scheduling_Electric_Vehicle_Fleets_as_a_Virtual_Battery_under_Uncertainty_using_Quantile_Forecasts.pdf).

**Not recommended as a near-term priority:** building full reactive-power/power-factor OPF modeling. Per Section 3.2, real-power-only dispatch matches accepted fleet-scheduling practice; the effort (new power-flow formulation, likely non-convex) is high and the payoff is concentrated in distribution-network-level studies, not depot-dispatch-level ones like GridPilot.

## Methodology Note

Research was conducted using `mcp__plugin_ecc_exa__web_search_exa` (primary) and `WebSearch` (secondary/cross-check), with `mcp__plugin_ecc_exa__web_fetch_exa` used to deep-read full text of the most load-bearing sources (WeaveGrid product/announcement pages, the DTU receding-horizon paper, the Shen et al. day-ahead/intraday MILP paper, the Kuvvetli et al. sequential-DP paper, the Hemmati et al. degradation-cost paper, the ORF DISCOM Dilemma article, and the Fleetvolts demand-charge article). Roughly 3-5 searches were run per sub-question across both engines. Sources skew toward 2024-2026 publications as requested; where an older or single-source claim is load-bearing, it is flagged inline (see WeaveGrid/Kaluza/Fermata vendor-material caveats in Section 1, the reactive-power percentage figures in Section 3.2, and the BRPL V2G pilot claim in Section 4.1). No primary DISCOM/CEA depot-specific smart-charging technical guidance document was found; this is called out as an explicit research gap in Section 4.2 rather than filled with inference. GridPilot's current-state architecture description was taken as given from the task brief and cross-referenced only at a high level against file names in the repository (`gridpilot/scheduler.py`, `gridpilot/ev_manager.py`, `gridpilot/depot_sim.py`, `pipeline/dvvnl_loader.py`) without re-deriving it from source code, per the task's framing of "current state" as already established context.
